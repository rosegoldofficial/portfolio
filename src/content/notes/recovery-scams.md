## How victims are found

- Leads sold or reused from the original scam: names, phone numbers, amounts lost.
- Forms on fake "victim registration" pages promoted through social media ads, sometimes styled as news reports.
- Public posts where victims describe their loss.

## The pitch

- Detailed knowledge of the original loss builds trust immediately.
- Authority claims: regulators, police units, Interpol, "blockchain forensics".
- A fee to release funds: tax, transfer fee, verification deposit, sometimes a request to install remote access software.

## Why it works

Victims are under financial and emotional pressure and want the loss to be reversible. Shame also stops them from asking family or their bank first.

## What helps

- **Clear public messaging** from regulators and banks that they never charge to return stolen money. In Poland, the financial supervisor and CSIRT KNF publish such warnings.
- **Bank-side flags** on customers with a recent fraud case, adding friction to new payees and crypto exchanges for some time afterwards.
- **Victim support** that includes a script: who to call, what legitimate recovery looks like, and that unsolicited offers are a red flag.

> **Takeaway:** a fraud case does not end with the first loss. Victims are a target list, and post-incident support should plan for the second contact.
